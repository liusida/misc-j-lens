                                                                                       A BSTRACT

                                                    Recent work from Anthropic introduced a new interpretability tool, called the
                                                    Jacobian lens (J-lens, Gurnee et al., 2026). This method identified linear directions
                                                    in the hidden states of frontier language models that had properties reminiscent
                                                    of working memory-like representations. However, it is unclear what mechanistic
                                                    properties of such features make them detectable to the J-lens. To this end, we
                                                    introduce a mathematical theory of the J-lens, and show that it preferentially identi-
                                                    fies coherent features – directions that act like leading, spatially coherent Lyapunov
                                                    modes. Small perturbations along these directions amplify across layers, remain
                                                    roughly constant across token positions, and are consistent across different contexts.
                                                    While we are agnostic to whether these properties are necessarily associated with a
                                                    “Global Workspace”, the analytically derived preferences of the J-lens nevertheless
                                                    explain prior empirical applied to both model classes. We validate our analysis on
                                                    a Bayes filter and a toy transformer, each optimally predicting next tokens emitted
                                                    from a Hidden Markov Model (HMM). We show how modulating task parameters
                                                    that change the coherence of features in the Bayes filter correspondingly and pre-
                                                    dictively modulates the response of the J-lens . We also introduce of a family of
                                                    J-lenses built to emphasize different temporal scales of information processing in
                                                    LLMs, which we call ω-J-lenses.

                                         1    I NTRODUCTION
                                         In a recent paper, Gurnee et al. (2026) introduced a novel interpretability technique that they claimed
                                         could identify an internal ‘workspace’ in large language models (LLMs), which had properties
                                         reminiscent of the Global Workspace (Baars, 1988; Dehaene et al., 1998). The object they primarily
                                         study is the ‘Jacobian lens’, or ‘J-lens’. This interpretability tool is designed in the spirit of other
                                         ‘lenses’ (nostalgebraist, 2020; Belrose et al., 2023), which seek to decode transformer hidden state
                                         activations into language by applying the unembedding matrix of the model to these activations
                                         (potentially after a learned affine map, as in the tuned lens). These lenses benefit interpretability by
                                         mapping hidden states to tokens, which are in the space of language and hopefully more interpretable.
                                         Decoding intermediate layers this way has shown that predictions are refined gradually across
                                         depth (nostalgebraist, 2020; Belrose et al., 2023), that multilingual models pass through an English-
                                         like latent space before producing the target language (Wendler et al., 2024), that false few-shot
                                         demonstrations derail predictions only after a critical layer (Halawi et al., 2024), and that a single
                                         hidden state already carries information about tokens several positions ahead (Pal et al., 2023). The
                                         trajectory of tuned-lens predictions across layers can also detect malicious inputs (Belrose et al.,
                                         2023).
                                         The Jacobian lens (J-lens, Gurnee et al., 2026) continues this line of work by introducing a new
                                         transfer operator onto the residual stream vector before the application of the unembedding matrix
                                         and mapping to tokens. This operator is the Jacobian of the transformer, averaged over tokens. In
                                         their paper, the motivation for the usage of the Jacobian K is based on standard results in calculus:
                                         A Jacobian describes how a small perturbation in the input of a function results in a change in the
                                         output. When the Jacobian is studied in an LLM, it provides directions in the residual stream that are

For transformers, depth is the evolution coordinate (T ) and token position the spatial coordinate.
A hydrodynamic interpretation requires identifying long wavelength with growth near zero. We’ll
show here that the J-space is tightly linked to Lyapunov theory. Under the assumptions below, the
J-lens favors spatially smooth, slowly varying responses, because it by construction integrates out the
fast dynamics, implicitly behaving like a low-pass filter in space. With this connection in hand, we
show that many of the results in the original paper follow naturally. Note that this is not an argument
that it does not identify a Global Workspace per se, but rather to shed light on what this method is
designed to capture and why that may or may not align with the philosophical conception of the
Global Workspace.

We call these the coherence parameters for directions in the Jacobian lens. We use the 2-norm for

magnitude, ρτ (v) is the lag coherence of a response, and ρc (v) is the context coherence. These
coherence parameters can be understood intuitively through the lens of spatiotemporal Lyapunov
modes: a describes the canonical notion of a Lyapunov exponent, here how a direction grows over
time (layers). ρτ describes how activity along a direction oscillates across spatial (token) positions,
with ρτ = 1 indicating no oscillation. Lastly, ρc describes how consistently direction is read in and
written out across different contexts (instantiations of Jacobian), given the same vector.
We illustrate what each coherence parameter measures in Fig. 2. Intuitively, magnitude measures the
total effect of a perturbation along a feature direction across both layers and time, thereby capturing
how persistent or relevant it is. Lag coherence measures how the response of a perturbation changes
across token positions, for which large values are approximately static, such as the overall topic
of a document, whereas incoherent features change rapidly, such as word type. Context coherence
measures whether a perturbation along a direction responds similarly in the same way for different
contexts, and how prior context modulates its effect. For example, adding in a new rule such as
“today is opposite day” into context requires reprocessing each feature with this in mind. Context-
coherent directions are processed consistently across contexts, while incoherent directions change
their behavior.

1.1    M APPING COHERENT MODES ONTO TOKENS

The analysis above characterizes the singular modes selected by Jacobian averaging, whereas the
J-space used in the original paper is constructed from token-indexed directions jq and is therefore not
simply the leading singular subspace of JH . These descriptions can nevertheless be connected exactly.
Let JH = U ΣV ∗ , and let wq denote the unembedding vector for token q. The corresponding J-lens
direction in the input residual stream is

Thus every token-indexed J-lens direction is a mixture of the right singular modes vi : the singular
value σi weights the strength of the corresponding coherent response channel, while ⟨ui , wq ⟩
measures how strongly that channel’s downstream response aligns with the token’s unembedding
direction. This connection matters because the J-space is operationally defined by approximating an
activation as a sparse non-negative combination of the directions jq (Gurnee et al., 2026). The sparse
decomposition therefore selects a small number of semantically labeled mixtures of the coherent
modes that survive averaging. In this sense, the singular structure of JH supplies the underlying
dynamical geometry, while the unembedding and sparse non-negative decomposition translate that
geometry into the token-level “concepts” reported by the J-lens.

1.2    FAMILIES OF JACOBIAN L ENSES

Our analysis suggests that the Jacobian lens can be modified to extract different types of information.
Here, we focus on the modulation of lag coherence selectivity. Eq. 7 highlights that averaging over
time points is equivalent to a Fourier projection onto the zero-frequency mode. Modulating the ω
parameter in this projection (or even generalizing to band-pass filtering) induces a family of lenses
we call ω-J-lenses, where ω ∈ [−π, π] given the Nyquist limit for sampling frequency 1 / token. We
demonstrate their selectivity in our toy task in Fig. 6.

Here λ is called the state persistence parameter, Dt ∈ {−1, 0, 1} with probabilities q, 1 − 2q, q and

this toy model is easily solved by a transformer, and indeed even the embeddings learn to represent
the latent states. The main result of our analysis is that we can a priori predict the order of the modes
and their approximate coherence parameters in the Jacobian lens of the transformer.

2.1   I NTRODUCING CONTEXTUAL DEPENDENCY

So far, all tokens across all random sequences for a given model are integrated the same way.
This means that the context coherence ρc of the Jacobian lens vectors cannot be modulated. To
introduce this, we add a new variable called a key state Wt ∈ {−1, +1} where the observed state
Yt = Xt + n2 1(Wt−1 = −1), and P (Wt = 1) = (1 + ψ)/2, with key bias ψ ∈ [0, 1]. This state is
always fully observed, forcing the models to look back one timestep to identify how to infer the state
of the observed token at the present timestep. This directly modulates context coherence because
it forces the read-in and read-out directions of a feature toQchange dependent on the context. Each
keyed state has its own independent key, making (1 + 2 i ni ) 2|K| tokens, where K is the set of
latents with keys.

The Jacobian lens on the transformer is defined by WU JH , where WU is the unembedding matrix.
Given that the activity after the unembedding layer maps to tokens via a softmax, the J-lens on the
transformer studies the effect of perturbations on the log probabilities, rather than the probabilities
of each state as in the Bayes filter. The difference on the Jacobian is a factor of ∂ log p(Xt ) =
∂p(Xt )/p(Xt ), which is an effective reweighting by a positive factor. This reweighting, however,
preserves the order of each factor (a, ρτ , ρc ), thus the qualitative ordering as predicted by the theory
should transfer to the transformer.


4     N UMERICAL R ESULTS

In the following three sections, we first introduce the parameter that is modulated across multiple
independent latent states in the HMM (three, across all sections) to induce selective changes in either
magnitude a, lag coherence ρτ , or context coherence ρc . We then apply the Bayes filter to validate
the analytical theory. Next, we train a two-layer decoder-only transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) with
residual dimension 128 and 4 heads per layer to predict the next token given the history (see Appendix
F for further details). Our primary result is a validation that the Jacobian lens unsupervisedly identifies
independent modes associated with the latent states, ordered by the selectively modulated coherence
factor. To do so, we fit an encoding model that maps from the Fourier modes (two per latent state
for sin and cos) of a latent to the residual stream, and compute the coherence parameters for these
directions. We also compute the total cosine similarity between this encoding subspace and the
Jacobian lens singular vectors vi (input side) and ui (output side) (Appendices G and H). We also
repeat this process on decoding vectors. Finally, we show that we can predict the persistence λ∗0 at
which two latents swap places, given different angular velocities θ.


4.1   S ELECTIVELY MODULATING MAGNITUDE

We first modulate the persistence λ by designing a hidden Markov model (HMM) with three latent
states, each on a ring, with λ = 0.8, 0.9, 0.95 respectively. We set the latent state velocity v = 0,
which holds ρτ and ρc fixed while selectively modulating the magnitude a of each latent (Fig. 4a).
All models learn to predict the next token as well as the optimal Bayes filter (Fig. 4b). On the Bayes
filter, the measured J-lens factors match the theory: the lens weight ||JH x||2 tracks the magnitude
a, while ρτ and ρc stay fixed (Fig. 4c). The J-lens applied to the transformer has similar factors at
each layer. As predicted, the J-lens directions identify latents in order of their magnitude a (Fig. 13).
Across all seeds, this is true for both the input direction vi (the right singular vector of the J-lens,
which reads in the residual stream at layer ℓ) and the output direction ui (the left singular vector,
which writes to the output). The rank order predicted by the theory is validated on the Bayes filter
and transformer (Figs. 11 and 12). The results are also consistent when utilizing decoding vectors for
each latent, rather than encoding vectors (Fig. 14), albeit with slightly smaller alignment values.

4.2         S ELECTIVELY MODULATING LAG COHERENCE

Next, we selectively modulated the lag coherence ρτ by changing the velocity v of each latent on the
ring (Fig. 5a). We placed states on a ring of 8 indices, and selected three latents again with velocities
v = 0 (perfectly coherent), 2, and 4 (maximally incoherent), that is, θ = 0, π/2, π. Our theory again
predicts that the J-lens should identify these latents in order of their lag coherence ρτ . As in the prior
section, we found that for transformers trained to optimality (Fig. 5b), this played out empirically
across all seeds (Fig. 15). The lag coherence ρτ of the J-lens applied to the transformer on each latent
separately was similarly modulated most by the velocity, while a and ρc were less sensitive (Fig.
5c). Again, the results are also consistent when utilizing decoding vectors for each latent, rather than
encoding vectors (Fig. 16).

4.2.1                      ω- LENS FAMILY
As described in Sec. 1.2, we also evaluated the ω-J-lenses on the lag coherence sweep for both
transformer layers and the Bayes filter. We swept the ω parameter across the whole range, and found
that it preferentially upweighted latent directions with lag coherence parameters (tuned via θ in the
task) that maximally aligned with the ω (Fig. 6). This suggests that the ω-J-lens family can be used
to selectively identify rapidly changing information across tokens (like syntax) or slowly changing
information, by sweeping ω.

4.3         S ELECTIVELY MODULATING CONTEXT COHERENCE

Lastly, we selectively modulate context coherence ρc by introducing a key state to latent states
with n = 2 states (Sec. 2.1). In this HMM, an observed state is flipped to the opposite state with
probability (1 − ψ)/2. Holding all other parameters besides ψ fixed, the context coherence can be
modulated independently, since ρc = ψ for the Bayes filter (Appendix F). We select ψi = 0, 0.75, 1,
ranging from maximally incoherent (0) to coherent (1), and train a transformer on this 3-latent HMM.
The J-lens factors of the Bayes filter match the theory, with ρc tracking ψ (Fig. 7). The Bayes filter
J-lens identifies the latents in the predicted order, and the transformer does as well (Fig. 17).

5    D ISCUSSION

We have shown that the Jacobian lens, as introduced by Gurnee et al. (2026), identifies modes in any
temporally and spatially varying dynamical system that jointly maximize three different statistical
features:

Magnitude a. Magnitude measures how a state grows or shrinks across a ‘temporal’ dimension. In
transformers, this growth is across layers, with depth exponent χ. However, this could be defined
differently in other systems. Magnitude aligns most directly with the standard notion of a Lyapunov
exponent, which typically measures growth across time in a dynamical system.

Lag coherence ρτ . Lag coherence measures how a feature is modulated across ‘spatial’ lags τ . In
transformers, this is measured across token positions, where a mode decays at rate µ and rotates at
frequency θ. Cross-token dynamics in language models have previously been studied as multi-particle
systems (Lu et al., 2019; Geshkovski et al., 2023), in which each token (particle) has a particular
spatial position and interacts with others via self-attention. The most coherent spatial modes are those
with zero spatial frequency and little decay (θ, µ ≈ 0). In Lyapunov theory, coherent directions relate
to the measurement of hydrodynamic Lyapunov modes, which are exactly the spatially coherent
Lyapunov vectors.

Context coherence ρc . Context coherence measures how much the dynamics are modulated across
different states or time periods. Roughly, maximizing context coherence identifies modes that are

architecture is highly context-dependent, whereas autonomous dynamical systems are not. That the
transformer learned features with much higher ρc than the Bayes filter for the context-incoherent
latent (Fig. 7c) suggests that transformers might learn to modulate contextual information via context-
independent dynamics, which could be a fundamental mechanism for symbolic processing. This
further suggests that it may be viable to study transformers more generally as dynamical systems
(Ostrow et al., 2024) just like recurrent neural networks (Sussillo & Barak, 2013).
Our analysis explains a number of phenomena in the original J-lens paper (Gurnee et al., 2026). As
described previously, high-magnitude, lag-coherent, and context-coherent directions do not have
to be large variance (Corollary 1.1). This is commensurate with Gurnee et al. (2026) finding that
the J-space is never more than 10% of activation variance. Lag coherence preference explains why,
when Claude was asked to ‘concentrate on X while solving Y’ (Gurnee et al., 2026), the J-lens
identified features corresponding to X, as these features would be prevalent and coherent across all
token positions. A preference for contextually coherent directions explains why averaging identifies
concepts that are broadly verbalizable, not specific to a single prompt.
Our work aligns with other results in mechanistic interpretability (Shai et al., 2024) that study how toy
transformers represent belief state geometry in their latent state and learn to decompose independent
factors of the generative process into orthogonal subspaces (Shai et al., 2026). However, this work
has generally relied on linear decoding of these belief states, which requires a priori knowledge of
the optimal belief states at each timepoint. Our results here suggest that the J-lens could be a way
to identify these belief states in an unsupervised manner, which would be effective for scaling to
real-world behaviors.
This analysis also suggests that a family of Jacobian lenses could be designed to capture different
types of information within transformers. For example, local information could be emphasized by
averaging over high-frequency Fourier modes, rather than low-frequency, as we showed in Fig. 6.
This could be relevant for isolating and understanding fast-changing information, such as those
on the level of syntax that change word-by-word. Contextually incoherent information could be
isolated by identifying Jacobian modes that minimize context coherence ρc , rather than maximize it.

Given recent work on estimating Jacobians from dynamical data (Eisen et al., 2026), the ω-J-lenses
could productively be applied to biological neural data as well. Together, our results clarify the
mathematical nature of the J-lens, which is necessary for such a useful and efficient interpretability
tool.

Limitations. Here, we only study toy models, in which the task was relatively easy to learn and the
coherence parameters of the task were easily manipulable.

Each model is a standard decoder-only transformer with causal self-attention and rotary position
embeddings (RoPE; Su et al., 2024) applied to the queries and keys only during attention. The
vocabulary size V is defined by the HMM: V = 83 + 1 = 513 for three latents on rings of 8 (every


                                                       20

combination of revealed places, plus the null token) and V = (1 + 2 · 23 ) 23 = 136 for the context

We write hℓ,t ∈ Rd for the residual stream at position t before block ℓ + 1: h0,t is the token

G     C OMPUTING ALIGNMENT OF J- LENS VECTORS AND LATENT STATE
      REPRESENTATIONS

We compute the Jacobian lens on 1000 sampled sequences from the task HMM after a 32-token
burn-in period, for sequences of length 192. We computed the Jacobian only on the tokens that
revealed latent states, totaling about 19,000 sources. We averaged over H = 128 lags. The Jacobian
was computed with forward-mode autodifferentiation and validated with a split-halves analysis. For
the Bayes filter, the input is separated by latent, and the output is the predicted distribution over each
latent separately as well.
We computed the singular value decomposition JH = U ΣV ∗ using standard linear algebra routines
in Python. The left singular vectors U are the output directions that the Jacobian writes, while the
right singular vectors V are the directions that the model reads. They are ordered by the singular
values on the diagonal of Σ.

On the output side, the patterns are directly readable from the tokens. Each token represents a
particular state of all latents, so we can directly align the Fourier mode patterns broadcast to the
output singular vectors. That is, each output singular vector is in RV , and for each Fourier mode of
each latent we construct a vector in RV by setting the entry of each token to cos(2πx/n) (or sin),
where x is the state of that latent in the token. Then, the encoding analysis simply comes down to
projection of the J-lens singular vector onto the plane spanned by each pattern individually.
We compute alignment by measuring how much a unit vector of the J-lens lies in the span of a group
of encoding or decoding directions, which in practice are taken to be the vectors corresponding to a
single mode of a latent state (typically two vectors corresponding to the sine and cosine directions).

I   VALIDATING SINGULAR VALUE SWITCHING THEORY
The switching analysis (Appendix E.1) makes two predictions. First, in the large-H limit the singular
value of each latent’s first Fourier mode is σi = |αi | ri / 1 − ri eiθi (Eq. (44)). Second, for a static
latent 0 with swept persistence λ0 and a fixed latent 1, the two singular values cross, and the top
J-lens direction switches from latent 1 to latent 0, at the persistence λ∗0 of Eq. (46). We test both
predictions on the Bayes filter, and the second on transformers trained across the switch.

Task. We use the toy-model HMM with two unkeyed latents on rings of n = 8 states, diffusion
probability q = 1/4 and reveal probability ε = 0.2, which gives V = 82 + 1 = 65 tokens. Latent 0
is static (v0 = 0) and its persistence λ0 is swept. Latent 1 has fixed persistence λ1 = 0.95 and
velocity v1 ∈ {1, 2, 3, 4}, that is, angular velocity θ1 = πv1 /4 ∈ {π/4, π/2, 3π/4, π}, which are all
the nonzero turns a ring of 8 allows, up to direction.

Theory. The predicted singular values are Eq. (44), evaluated for each latent on mode j = 1. The
predicted switch point is Eq. (46), which depends only on latent 1 and the shared parameters, not
on λ0 . The series for Ec |Sj,N | (Eq. (43)), needed for the individual coherences but not for σi , is
truncated at 200 terms.

Measuring the singular values. We compute the J-lens as in Appendix G, on 1000 sampled
sequences of 192 tokens with a 32-token burn-in, using only the revealed source tokens, and sum

Switch point. For each θ1 we measure σ0 and σ1 at seven values of λ0 , spaced 0.02 apart and
